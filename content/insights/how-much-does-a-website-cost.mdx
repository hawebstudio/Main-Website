# How Much Does a Website Cost?

Website costs vary significantly depending on scope, functionality, design requirements, integrations, and level of customization — there is no single fixed number that applies to every business. A simple brochure site built on a template starts cheap; a custom-designed, conversion-focused business website or an ecommerce build with integrations costs meaningfully more, because the work involved is fundamentally different, not because of arbitrary pricing tiers.

Rather than quote a number that won't apply to your situation, it's more useful to understand exactly what drives cost up or down — so you can budget realistically and evaluate quotes from any agency, including ours.

## What Actually Drives the Cost

**Design complexity.** A template with light customization costs far less than a custom design built around your brand, content, and conversion goals. Custom design means original layouts, interaction and animation work, and a design system built for your business rather than adapted from someone else's.

**Number and type of pages.** More pages generally means more cost, but page *type* matters more than page *count* — a pricing calculator, a booking flow, or a filterable case study index takes meaningfully longer to build than five static content pages.

**Frontend technology and CMS.** A basic WordPress or Webflow site is typically faster and cheaper to build than a custom Next.js application, but trades off performance, flexibility, and long-term scalability. See [Shopify vs WooCommerce](/insights/shopify-vs-woocommerce) for how this trade-off plays out specifically for ecommerce platforms.

**Ecommerce functionality.** Product catalogs, cart and checkout flows, payment gateway integration, inventory management, and shipping logic add real engineering work on top of a standard website build.

**Integrations.** CRM connections, booking systems, payment gateways, authentication, email marketing platforms, and analytics all add setup and testing time — each one is effectively its own small project.

**Content and creative.** Copywriting, photography, video, and custom illustration are often the most underestimated line items in a website budget. A visually polished design with no real content behind it does not perform.

**SEO and technical SEO.** Site structure, on-page optimization, structured data, and technical SEO setup planned from day one is meaningfully cheaper than retrofitting SEO onto a site that wasn't built with it in mind.

**Performance and analytics.** Image optimization, Core Web Vitals work, and analytics/tracking setup (GA4, conversion tracking) affect both cost and the site's ability to actually generate leads once live.

**Hosting, deployment, and ongoing support.** The build itself is a one-time cost; hosting, maintenance, security updates, and support are ongoing and should be budgeted separately from the start.

**Migration.** Moving an existing site to a new platform or redesigning around existing content and SEO equity is its own category of work, distinct from building from scratch.

## Typical Categories of Website

These categories reflect genuinely different scopes of work, not fixed packages:

- **Simple brochure / business website** — a small number of pages, primarily informational, minimal custom functionality.
- **Professional business website** — custom design, more pages, stronger SEO foundation, built to represent an established business credibly.
- **Conversion-focused website** — built specifically around lead generation or sales, with copy, structure, and design decisions driven by conversion goals rather than aesthetics alone. See [Low Conversion Rate](/problems/low-conversion-rate) if this is the core problem you're solving for.
- **Ecommerce website** — product catalog, cart, checkout, payments, and typically inventory or order management.
- **Custom web application** — bespoke functionality beyond a standard content site: portals, dashboards, booking systems, or workflows specific to the business.

## Common Mistakes When Budgeting for a Website

Comparing quotes on price alone without comparing scope is the most common mistake — a quote that's a fraction of another's is usually excluding something, whether that's content, SEO, revisions, or post-launch support. Underestimating ongoing costs (hosting, maintenance, content updates) leads to a site that degrades after launch. Skipping content preparation pushes the real cost of the project later rather than removing it, and choosing a platform based on upfront price rather than long-term fit often means a more expensive rebuild sooner than planned.

## What to Ask an Agency Before Committing

Before comparing prices, ask what's actually included: design revisions, number of pages, CMS training, SEO setup, analytics setup, content entry, post-launch support, and hosting. Ask what would increase the price — additional pages, custom functionality, extra integrations — and ask who owns the code, content, and domain once the project is finished. A vague answer to any of these is a bigger red flag than the price itself.
